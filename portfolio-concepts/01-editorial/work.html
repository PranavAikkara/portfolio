Casework — four write-ups

What I built,
and what it cost
to make it real.

Each of these ran in production for real users. These are the notes I wish I'd had going in.

Case 01 — 2025

Vectorless retrieval over
enterprise documents.

Most teams reach for a vector database on day one. We didn't, and answers got more trustworthy, not less.

The premise: instead of chopping documents into chunks and hoping cosine similarity finds the right one, let the model navigate the document the way a person does. It reads a structured outline, decides which sections are relevant, and opens only those.

The payoff is traceability. Every answer points to a page, not to an opaque embedding. When a stakeholder asks "where did this come from," there is a real, checkable citation. The cost is a careful indexing pass up front and a model good enough to reason over structure.

What surprised me. The win wasn't accuracy on easy questions, it was honesty on hard ones. The system says "not in these documents" instead of confidently inventing a passage.

Where it strains. Documents with no structure at all. If there's no outline to navigate, you have to build one first, which is its own project.

Case 02 — 2025

Voice agents that hold
a real conversation.

The speech models are solved. Everything around them is where the project actually lives.

A caller picks up, talks over the agent, trails off mid-sentence, gives the answer to a question two steps ahead. A scripted bot falls apart here. The work was building a dialogue manager that tracks state, handles barge-in, and keeps latency low enough that the pauses feel human.

Latency is the tyrant. Every component, recognition, reasoning, synthesis, eats a slice of a budget the caller measures in awkward silence. Most of the engineering was shaving milliseconds and choosing where a fast-but-rough answer beats a slow-but-perfect one.

If the caller notices the silence, you've already lost them. Field note — latency budget review
Case 03 — 2024

Ingestion for documents
that fight back.

Clean data is a myth. The pipeline's whole job is to manufacture it.

Scanned at an angle. A table whose cells were merged by someone in a hurry. Two languages sharing a paragraph. The ingestion layer normalises structure before it ever looks at meaning, so the model downstream receives clean, ordered text instead of a salvage operation.

The principle I kept returning to: fix layout first, content second. Get the reading order right and most of the "AI" problems downstream turn out to have been data problems all along.

Rule of thumb. If a human can't tell the reading order at a glance, the parser won't either. That page needs a different strategy, not a better prompt.

Case 04 — 2024

Fine-tuning a small model
to do one thing well.

A frontier model for a narrow task is a rounding error of capability and a fortune in latency.

For a tightly-scoped classification job, I curated a dataset, fine-tuned a small open model, and got a system that was faster, cheaper, and fully under our control. The interesting part was the data: synthetic examples helped breadth but quietly skewed the distribution until the eval caught it.

Knowing when to stop mattered more than any hyperparameter. Once the model cleared the bar a human reviewer would, more training was just polishing a corner nobody looked at.
